This document teaches how to write B-Roll style short-form video scripts for social media. It is written to be used by an AI tool as a standing reference: read it before writing any B-Roll script, and run the self-check at the end before delivering one.

The guidance here is general-purpose. It works for any professional who posts short-form video content. Examples lean on real estate (agents, loan officers, title companies, property managers, home inspectors) because it is a content-heavy, trust-driven industry where this format performs well, but nothing here is locked to one company, one person, or one brand voice. Swap in any subject-matter expert and the structure holds.

---

## 1. What a B-Roll video is

"B-Roll" in the short-form content sense refers to a video built around **on-screen text over cutaway footage**, narrated by a **caption written as voice-over**. The viewer does not see the creator talking to camera. Instead, they see a short text hook while the caption plays (as a voice-over recording, a text-to-speech track, or simply as the written caption itself if the platform doesn't support audio), and then they open the caption to read the full piece.

This distinguishes B-Roll from other common short-form formats:

| Format | What the viewer sees | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Talking Head | Creator speaking to camera | Client stories, myth corrections, narrative with an arc |
| Green Screen | Creator reacting to an article/data on screen | News reactions, countering a headline with real numbers |
| B-Roll | Cutaway footage + on-screen text + caption | Philosophy, positioning, personal brand, routines, "here's how this works" education |
| Carousel | Static slides, swiped one at a time | Stepwise teaching, transparency plays, checklists |

**Use B-Roll when:** the content is more explain-y than story-driven, doesn't need the creator's face to land, and works as something the viewer reads as much as watches. Myth-busting, industry education, philosophy/mindset content, and "here's what's changing" announcements are the sweet spot.

**Don't use B-Roll when:** the content has a strong narrative arc with dialogue beats (that's Talking Head) or is reacting to a specific piece of external news/data (that's Green Screen, which should show the source on screen).

## 2. The two-part structure

Every B-Roll script has exactly two parts:

```
ON-SCREEN TEXT: [what appears on screen, 3 lines]
CAPTION: [the full written piece, functions as voice-over and as the post caption]
```

The on-screen text is the **tease**. The caption is the **payoff**. They do different jobs and must never do the same job twice.

---

## 3. On-screen text: building the tease

The on-screen text is not a title. It is not a summary of the caption. It is a three-line structure designed to make someone stop scrolling and then tap to read more.

### The three-line shape

**Line 1, the setup.** A short line establishing the situation, the credential, or the surprising claim. This line ends in an ellipsis (`…`), signaling that more is coming.

**Line 2, the twist.** A second line that sharpens the setup: the contrast, the stakes, or the specific detail that raises the question in the viewer's mind.

**Line 3 (after a blank line), the tease.** A line that names *what the caption will deliver* without delivering it. This line ends in a colon (`:`). It promises a payoff, "here's how," "here's why," "here's what changed", that the caption must answer.

### Generic template

```
[Setup: situation or credential]…
[Twist: the contrast or the stakes]

Here's [what/how/why] [the promise]:
```

### Real estate examples

```
ON-SCREEN TEXT:
My client's inspection came back with 14 issues…
The seller's agent said the deal was dead.

Here's how it closed anyway:
```

```
ON-SCREEN TEXT:
Rocket pre-approved my buyer in 6 minutes…
Underwriting denied it 3 weeks later.

Here's what actually went wrong:
```

```
ON-SCREEN TEXT:
I've closed over 500 loans…
If I started over today with zero referrals, I wouldn't do it alone.

Here's what I'd do instead:
```

### Rules for on-screen text

- **Keep each line short.** Aim for roughly 4–8 words per line. This is on-screen text, read in under two seconds per line; it is not a sentence meant to be studied.
- **The tease line must promise something specific**, not a vague "here's why that matters." A viewer should be able to guess *the category* of the payoff (a method, a reason, a number, a turning point) without knowing the payoff itself.
- **Never let the on-screen text do the caption's job.** If someone could read only the on-screen text and walk away with the full insight, the tease has failed, it gave away the free sample and the entrée at once.
- **One idea, not two.** Don't try to tease two different insights in three lines. Pick the strongest angle and cut the rest.

## 4. The caption: delivering the payoff

The caption is the full piece: what plays as voice-over, and what a viewer reads if they open the post. It has its own internal structure.

### Caption structure

1. **Opening line: answers the tease immediately, with new information.** Do not restate the on-screen text. The viewer already read it. Give them the next layer: the specific mechanism, the number, the moment.
2. **Context: who, what, when, in plain language.** Just enough detail to ground the story or claim. No throat-clearing, no "let me tell you about a time when."
3. **The turn: the myth corrected, the mechanism explained, or the lesson landed.** This is the actual value of the piece. It should be concrete enough that someone could act on it or repeat it to someone else.
4. **The wider point: why this matters beyond this one instance.** Zoom out from the specific example to the general principle, especially if the piece is meant to build authority or shift how the audience thinks about a category (not just this one situation).
5. **Soft close: a question, an invitation, or nothing at all.** B-Roll captions rarely need a hard call-to-action. A rhetorical question, an offer to help, or just letting the last line land is usually stronger than "link in bio."

### Length and pacing

- Caption length: roughly **120–280 words**, depending on platform and topic density. Long enough to deliver real value, short enough to read in under 60–90 seconds.
- **Vary sentence length deliberately.** Write two or three sentences of setup, then land on one short, declarative sentence. That rhythm is what makes a caption feel spoken rather than written. Example: "The underwriter said it couldn't be done. It could be done." Long sentence, then a hard stop.
- **One reenacted dialogue beat, when the story has one.** "She asked how the numbers felt. I didn't say anything." A single quoted or paraphrased exchange makes a caption feel like a real memory instead of a summary.

### Real estate example (full script)

```
ON-SCREEN TEXT:
My client's inspection came back with 14 issues…
The seller's agent said the deal was dead.

Here's how it closed anyway:

CAPTION:
Fourteen issues on the report. Foundation crack, old wiring, a roof with
maybe three years left. The seller's agent told my buyer's agent to walk,
that no lender would touch it.

Here's what most agents don't know. A lender declines a house when its
problems create risk nobody's accounted for.

So we did the accounting. We got repair estimates on the big items, built
them into the negotiation as a price reduction instead of a repair
request, and structured the loan so the buyer had cash left over to fix
the roof in year one instead of year five.

The deal everyone called dead closed in 31 days.

The plan was the missing piece all along.

If your buyer's inspection report looks like a crime scene, that's the
start of a different conversation.
```

## 5. How the tease and the caption relate to each other

This is the part most first drafts get wrong, so it gets its own section.

### Rule: the on-screen text and the caption's opening line must never say the same thing twice

If the on-screen text already promises "here's how it closed anyway," the caption's first line cannot be "the deal closed anyway." That's not a payoff, that's an echo. The caption's opening line has to deliver the *next* layer of information, the mechanism, not the outcome the tease already implied.

**Test:** could someone who only read the on-screen text skip the caption's first line and lose nothing? If yes, rewrite the caption's opening.

### Rule: a flat fact is not a tease

"An agent closed a hard deal" is a fact. It is not a tease, because it doesn't raise a question. A real tease creates a specific, answerable question in the viewer's head: *how* did it close, *why* did the lender say no, *what* changed between the denial and the approval. Lead with the number, the contradiction, or the turning point, not the plain summary of what happened.

Weak: `My client's loan almost fell through…`
Strong: `Underwriting denied my client twice in one week…`

The second version is specific enough to make someone ask "wait, twice? what happened?" The first is just a mood.

### Rule: establish pronoun antecedents inside the caption itself

Because the on-screen text and the caption are separate pieces of text, and because a viewer might see one without carefully reading the other, or the platform's caption might get truncated, **the caption cannot assume the on-screen text already told the viewer who "she," "he," or "they" refers to.** If the caption's opening line uses a pronoun with no noun anywhere in that same sentence or the sentence right before it, the viewer has no subject.

Broken: `She was collecting $500 a month, until one call changed that.` (Who is "she"? Nothing before this line names her.)

Fixed: `A grandmother was collecting $500 a month from her son, until one call changed that.`

Name the person, or their role, "a first-time buyer," "my client," "an agent I work with," "a builder", in the same breath the pronoun first appears. Never rely on the reader having perfectly retained the on-screen text.

## 6. Voice and tone (general principles)

These principles apply regardless of whose voice the script is written in. The specific vocabulary, catchphrases, and personality of any individual creator should layer on top of this foundation, not replace it.

- **Translate jargon into plain language, every time.** "Debt-to-income ratio" becomes "how much of your paycheck is already spoken for." "Non-warrantable condo" becomes "a building that doesn't qualify for standard financing." Explain the concept before or instead of using the term of art, never assume the audience already knows it.
- **Write like someone talks, not like someone types.** Contractions, sentence fragments for emphasis, and a conversational cadence read as authentic. A caption that sounds like a blog post excerpt has already lost the format.
- **Use rhetorical questions as punctuation, not as filler.** "Who wants to guess what happened next?" lands once, hard. Overused, it reads like a template.
- **Include a fair-minded caveat before a counter-argument.** "Buy-before-you-sell programs are real, and they work great for the right buyer", then explain the catch. Acknowledging the other side first is what makes the correction land as expertise instead of a sales pitch.
- **Land on short declarative sentences.** After two or three sentences of buildup, cut to something short and flat. "That's the myth." "It closed anyway." "The plan was the missing piece." Keep them positive statements; do not use the denial-then-correction pair (see SKILL.md language rules). This is the single biggest lever for making a caption sound spoken rather than written.
- **Close soft.** A question to the audience, an offer to help, or simply stopping, all outperform a hard sell. Reserve explicit calls-to-action ("DM me," "comment below," a reply keyword) for pieces specifically built to capture leads.

## 7. Sourcing and credibility rules

Short-form video is a trust business. A single invented number or unverifiable claim, once caught, costs more credibility than a dozen good posts earn back. Apply these rules regardless of topic:

- **Every number, client detail, and specific claim must come from a verified source**: a real transcript, a documented case, a cited statistic, or explicit confirmation from the person the content is written for. Never invent a number, a dollar figure, or a client detail to make a script land better. If a detail is missing, say so and ask, rather than filling the gap with something plausible-sounding.
- **Never disparage a named competitor or category of competitor.** Frame gaps in what others offer as differences in specialty or systems, not incompetence: "that's not what they specialize in," not "they don't know what they're doing." This keeps the content sounding confident rather than bitter, and avoids reputational and legal risk.
- **Don't reuse a story that's already been published without flagging it.** If a content calendar or index exists, check it before reusing a client story or example. Reusing a story is sometimes fine (evergreen education can repeat), but it should be a deliberate choice, not an accident.

## 8. Choosing the real estate angle

Within real estate, B-Roll content tends to cluster into a few reliable content categories. Any of these can carry the format:

- **Myth correction.** "Everyone thinks X. Here's what's actually true." (e.g., "A pre-approval from a call center isn't the same as an underwritten approval.")
- **Mechanism reveal.** "Here's what actually happens behind the scenes when X." (e.g., how a non-warrantable condo gets priced, how an appraisal gap actually gets resolved.)
- **Industry-change announcement.** "A rule/guideline/market condition is changing. Here's what that means for you." (e.g., new condo reserve requirements, a rate environment shift.)
- **Personal brand / philosophy.** A belief, a piece of advice, or a mindset shift, illustrated with one concrete moment rather than abstract advice. (e.g., what changed after attending an industry event, a piece of coaching advice that changed how someone runs client meetings.)
- **Client story with a twist.** A deal that looked dead, or a client who almost made an expensive mistake, resolved through specific action. (e.g., the 14-issue inspection example above.)

Pick the category the source material actually supports. Don't force a client anecdote into a philosophy post, or stretch a genuine mechanism explanation into a personal story it isn't.

## 9. Full example set

### Example A: Mechanism reveal (loan officer / lender angle)

```
ON-SCREEN TEXT:
Another "pre-approval" just got denied…
The buyer never saw it coming.

Here's why that keeps happening:

CAPTION:
Your buyer gets pre-approved online in six minutes. You write the offer.
It gets accepted. Then, three weeks later, underwriting says no.

Here's what most people don't realize about how the big call-center
lenders work. The person who issued that pre-approval wasn't an
underwriter. They were a rep reading a script, running numbers through a
system, and moving to the next file. If a guideline changed last month,
or a detail on the application was slightly off, they might not catch
it. The real review only happens once the file lands in underwriting,
which by then is weeks into the transaction.

For a high-volume lender, a percentage of files falling out of contract
is an acceptable cost of doing business at that scale. For your buyer,
it's the house they already fell in love with.

An eleventh-hour denial is what happens when nobody actually looked at
the file until it was almost too late.

Ask who reviews the file before you write the offer.
```

### Example B: Industry-change announcement (works for agents, lenders, or title companies)

```
ON-SCREEN TEXT:
New condo financing rules start in August…
Most agents haven't heard yet.

Here's what's changing and what to do now:

CAPTION:
Two changes are landing this year that will affect almost every condo
sale. First, the reduced-documentation review option is going away
entirely. That used to let buyers skip half the paperwork by putting
down 10% or more. Now, every loan gets the full review, no shortcuts.

Second, the minimum reserve requirement for HOAs is jumping from 10% to
15% of annual assessments. A building collecting a million dollars a
year in dues now needs $150,000 sitting in reserves instead of
$100,000. Buildings that haven't been saving enough are about to become
harder to finance, overnight.

If you have a condo listing coming up, or a past client sitting in a
building that hasn't been paying attention to this, now is the time to
get them talking to their HOA board. Waiting until a buyer is under
contract is how deals fall apart in the final week.

The building doesn't have to be ready today. It has to start moving in
that direction before someone tries to sell.
```

### Example C: Personal brand / philosophy (works for any real estate professional)

```
ON-SCREEN TEXT:
I got told I had "too much energy" on stage…
A man doing the same thing gets called confident.

Here's why I stopped apologizing for it:

CAPTION:
I gave a talk a few months ago and got feedback afterward that I came
across as too much. Too sharp, too direct, too sure of myself for a
room full of strangers.

Here's the thing I couldn't stop thinking about. A man saying the exact
same things, in the exact same tone, walks away being called decisive.
A woman does it and there's suddenly a different word for it.

I'm not bringing this up to complain. I'm bringing it up because I know
I'm not the only one who's heard some version of it.

Standing behind what you believe, clearly and without hedging, gets a
different reaction depending on who's saying it.

I'd rather be direct and occasionally misread than quiet and
comfortable.
```

## 10. Self-check before delivering any B-Roll script

Run through this list before finalizing any script:

1. **On-screen text is exactly three lines**: setup ending in an ellipsis, twist, then a blank line and a tease ending in a colon.
2. **The tease promises something specific**, not a vague "here's why that matters."
3. **The caption's opening line delivers new information**, not a restatement of the on-screen text.
4. **Every pronoun in the caption's opening has an antecedent in the same sentence or the one before it.**
5. **Every number and specific detail is verified** against a real source, not invented.
6. **No competitor or category of competitor is disparaged.** Gaps are framed as differences in specialty, not incompetence.
7. **Jargon is translated into plain language** or removed entirely.
8. **At least one sentence in the caption is short and declarative**, landing after a longer buildup.
9. **The close is soft** unless the piece is specifically built to capture leads.
10. **Read it aloud.** If a sentence sounds like it was written for a page rather than said out loud, rewrite it.
