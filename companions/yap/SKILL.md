# YAP

Turn someone's raw insight into a ready-to-record yap video.

**The core truth:** good yaps look off-the-cuff but are engineered. Your job is to build the
structure while keeping the language so conversational nobody would guess it was written.

Built from a study of 100 true-yap outlier videos (1,324 scraped; dialogue skits, lifestyle
vlogs, and meme clips classified out) across 14 creators — mortgage, real estate, marketing,
and content-creation coaches — every rule below is evidence-backed.
**A yap is one creator talking directly TO THE VIEWER, start to finish.** Acted roleplay/skits
and vlogs are different formats and are out of scope for this skill. See `references/evidence.md` for the data behind any claim.

---

## THE FRAMEWORK

> # 1 CLAIM → 2 HOOKS → 3 PILLARS → LAND IT

Four moves. Always these four, in this order. Everything else is detail.

---

## STEP 0 — Mine the input

Read whatever they give you — bullets, a transcript, a rant, half an idea — and pull out:
the topic, the **take** (do they have an opinion or just information?), any story or client
moment, the emotion, and the one thing a viewer should remember.

**Don't ask which mode they want — decide.** Produce an **outline** (cues per pillar) unless
they say they ramble, freeze on camera, or ask for a script — then produce **word-for-word**.
A word-for-word script is delivered read-and-repeat (script just off-frame; memorize one line,
say it to camera 2–3 ways, cut the gaps) — never read aloud, or they'll sound like they're
reading and viewers feel it instantly.

**Default to outline.** Only offer word-for-word if they say they ramble, freeze, or want a
script. **Default length: 60 seconds or less** — that is the beginner rule and it applies unless
they say they already post regularly, in which case 60–90s. Don't ask anything else — every
other decision is yours.

---

## STEP 1 — ONE CLAIM

The topic comes from within — their business, their life, their mind, their heart. Being
*inspired* by someone else's video is fine; borrow the **structure**, never the content — the
spin, the story and the numbers have to be theirs. **If the
insight could come out of anyone else's mouth, it isn't theirs yet** — dig for the version only
they can say. Then find the single argument the video makes and run it through three gates:

**Gate 1 — Would someone argue with it? (the sharpener)**
Gates 2 and 3 are hard requirements; this one is the difference between good and great —
a concrete single-claim video can work without a hot take, but its ceiling drops.
If everyone already agrees, it's information, not insight. Information is free now; nobody
watches a yap to learn generic facts. Find the take hiding in the notes: why do they care?
What would they defend?

**Gate 2 — Can they see it? (non-negotiable)**
Can the viewer picture it in their mind — an object, a number, a moment, a document, a
sentence someone actually said? **Never a concept you invented.** In the study, "your mindset
is costing you opportunities" scored 0.2x; the same creator's story about his dad tapping an
empty glass scored 87x. If the topic is a noun phrase you made up ("the confidence tax"),
you don't have a video yet — go find the object.

**Gate 3 — Is it ONE claim? (non-negotiable)**
One argument, one insight. Pillars *support* that claim — but a second **claim** confuses the
viewer about what the video is even for. Exactly one. Videos with a single claim reached 24–87x; videos with genuinely separate points
plateaued at 5–14x. Three lessons in the notes = three videos. Say so, and offer to script
the others.

If the notes are an info-dump with no POV and you can't find a take, **do not format the
dump.** Tell them what's missing and ask one sharp question ("What made you want to talk about
this?" usually works), or propose 2–3 candidate angles for them to pick.

**Sharpening the claim — four switches. Flip at least two:**
- **Object, not conclusion** — open on the thing, not the lesson.
- **Trap, not option** — "Don't do X, do Y" beats "you could also do Y." (26.8x vs 2.4x)
- **Cost** — reveal what helps the viewer at your own or your industry's expense. The
  confessions outperform the reassurances.
- **Sort** — name who exactly this is being done to, so the qualifying group knows it's them.

## STEP 2 — TWO HOOKS

Two hooks, two audiences, same three seconds. Most of the feed scrolls **muted** — the
on-screen text is the only hook they meet. The voice is the hook for whoever stopped.

**Why both matter:** Instagram measures **skip rate** — how many people swipe away inside the
first three seconds. A high skip rate stops distribution cold. Hooks are not decoration.

### The audio hook (first sentence, ~15–20 words, ~5 seconds)

- **Open with the claim — the argument, the contrarian take.** Give away the conclusion,
  withhold the pillars.
- **Spark curiosity, intrigue, or emotion** by aiming the line at one person's **fear, concern,
  want, or need**. That specific person is where the feeling comes from — a line aimed at
  "everyone" lands on no one. It is the most important sentence in the video: written to be said like
  they mean it.
- **Statement, not question.** Zero of the top-third openers end in a question; 18% of the
  bottom third do. The most reliable first-sentence signal in the corpus.
- **Second person early.** Corpus-wide, "you" lands about once every 20 words; the first
  sentence usually has it, though story-driven yaps may open in first person and hand over.
- **Longer than a fragment.** Typical first sentence: 15–20 words. The weak ones are short
  and empty ("We're here in Irvine!"). Length spent on the *claim* is good; length spent on
  *conditions* is not.
- **Never open with:** a greeting, your location, your credentials, or a hedge.

Write **3 hook options** so they can pick what sounds like them.

### The visual hook (the PARTNER to the audio hook — never a subtitle)

74% of true-yap outliers carry designed on-screen text (median 8.7x vs 5.1x without). Pick
the pairing:

| If the audio... | The text should... | Example |
|---|---|---|
| opens a **story/scene** | state the **thesis** the story is withholding | speech: "I came to America from a war…" · text: **"How you look matters more than you think"** |
| states the **claim** | **compress** it to ≤8 words | text: **"The secret about loud people"** |
| names an exact **situation** | gate it: "When you…" / "If you…" so the qualifying viewer knows it's them | **"Bigger downpayment"** over a myth-busting explainer |
| **is already the claim in ≤8 words** | repeat it **verbatim** — it reads fine muted | one creator runs this on every video; repeats sit behind 190x and 144x outliers |

The failure mode isn't repeating — it's transcribing a *weak* opener. If the first line wouldn't
survive as a headline, it doesn't belong on screen.

**Mechanics:** ≤8 words · top-center · sentence case · high-contrast card or outline · on
screen at frame one and held for the first ~3 seconds. One emphasized word max.

**The mute test:** mute the video — does the on-screen text stop the scroll? Unmute — does the
voice hold? Both must pass independently.

**Bonus third layer:** if they have a real artifact — a document, receipt, chart, screenshot,
news headline — tell them to hold it up or greenscreen it. It proves evidence is coming in a
way text can't fake. The biggest outliers stack face + text + artifact.

## STEP 3 — THREE PILLARS

The middle holds up the one claim, never adds new ones. These are three KINDS of pillar —
a menu, not a checklist. **One strong pillar can hold the whole video up; three is the ceiling.**
Pick what the notes support:

- **A story** — something that happened, with a person and a place. Concrete and sensory.
- **A number** — worked all the way through to its consequence. One scenario beats a table:
  *"An extra 5% down is $25k on a $500k house — and it only lowers your payment by $154."*
- **A conversation, retold** — the exchange they keep having, told straight to camera
  ("I just had to tell a couple they're priced out"). They stay themselves — no acting parts.

**Rules for the middle:**
- **Make it concrete.** One story OR one worked number per pillar — never abstract, never both.
- **Re-hook between pillars.** Each re-hook is one short line that resets attention and buys
  the next 20 seconds. Write one before pillar 2 and one before pillar 3:
  *"here's what nobody tells you" · "and the craziest part" · "number three is the one that
  matters" · "here's why."*
- **Pre-empt the objection at ~60% through**, not at the top: *"now before you come at me and
  say [objection] — that's a different scenario, because…"*
- **Credentials, if any: right after the hook — roughly 10–15 seconds in — one sentence, flagged
  as an interruption** ("but first — I'm X, I've done Y"). Never first. Zero videos that led with
  credentials scored above 3.4x.
- **Scope limit near the end** — "now this doesn't work in every case, if you're X then Y" —
  buys enormous credibility.

---

## STEP 4 — LAND IT

**The hook and the last line get written before recording.** The hook earns attention; the last
line is the **guardrail** — it keeps them from rambling and is their **cue to stop speaking**.
They say it, and they're done.

Three ways to land:
- **The punchline** — a compressed callback to the opening image. *"Empty makes noise, full has
  weight."*
- **The question to comments** — *"So which one annoys you the most?"* (Top performers using
  this ask for nothing else — no follow ask at all.)
- **The open loop** — *"Part two is where most people mess up."*

**The CTA rides on the payoff — it never replaces it.** Land the line, *then* ask for the
follow. A hard link-in-bio CTA is fine and converts, but caps reach; use it when the video is
doing a job, not when it's doing reach.

**Never end with:** an applause line ("we'll see you next time"), or a bare instruction with no
payoff behind it.

## LANGUAGE RULES

Write how people talk, not how they write:

- **"You" about every 20 words.** The densest signal in the whole study — 1 in 21 words.
- **Cut "I" before adding "you."** The worst-performing creator was the most self-referential.
  Use first person to earn the claim, then hand the sentence over.
- **Vary sentence length violently.** Median 10 words, but 38% under 8 and 20% over 20. Land
  points by collapsing from a long sentence into three short ones. That collapse *is* the shout.
- **Contract everything.** 84% contraction rate. Save the uncontracted form for emphasis:
  "**Do not** send that money."
- **Strip** like, you know, I mean, literally, basically, right? — weak performers use 2–4x more.
  **Keep** so, and, but at the start of sentences. Connectives are structure; hedges are leakage.
- **Define jargon in six words and keep moving:** *"something called X, which is basically Y."*
  Never apologize for complexity before delivering it.
- **Every number gets** a second unit ("2% — which is $8,000"), a rounding hedge ("like a
  hundred bucks"), or a reaction ("Crazy, I know").
- Use their own phrases from the notes wherever possible. That's their voice.
- Read it back: if a sentence would sound odd said out loud to a friend, rewrite it.

## BENCHMARKS

- **~200 words per minute** — ~300 words for a 60–90 second yap (corpus medians: 204 wpm, 258 words).
- **New yappers: default to ≤60 seconds (~200 words).** A hard cap is a training wheel — it forces
  sharpness and keeps recording and editing manageable while they build reps.
- **Length does not predict performance**, so this is a beginner constraint, not a law: median
  outlier yap is 81 seconds, and 20-second and 3-minute yaps both win (band medians 6.9–8.6x, no
  trend). Once they have reps, talk exactly as long as the pillars take, then land.

## RECORDING NOTES (include when the user seems nervous or new)

These lower the barrier — the difference between a yap made and a yap avoided:

- **Loose script, not word-for-word.** Plan the hook and the pillars, then talk. Word-for-word
  makes people sound like they're reading and viewers feel it instantly. (This is why outline
  mode is the default.)
- **Record each pillar 3–5 times.** The first take is warmup; the last usually sounds the most
  human. Keep that one. A yap is not a one-take performance.
- **Edit heavily, no shame.** Nailing a yap in one take is an advanced skill. Cut the dead air
  and keep it moving.
- **FaceTime voice.** Talk like you're on a call with your best friend, nobody else watching.
- **Starting out: cap it at 60 seconds.** Until they have reps in, a one-minute limit makes them
  sharper and makes recording and editing far easier. Graduate to longer once it feels natural.
- **You will suck first.** It takes roughly 100 yaps before you stop cringing at yourself. Post
  the bad ones anyway.

## OUTPUT TEMPLATE

The output must mirror the framework the user was taught: **1 CLAIM → 2 HOOKS → 3 PILLARS →
LAND IT**. Use those words as the output headings — never "beats", "points", or "steps" in
what you hand the user.
Use exactly this. Outline and script mode share the identical headings — only the contents of
the Pillars change (cues vs word-for-word):

```
# [Working title — what the video is about in plain words]

**Runtime:** ~Xs · **Where to film:** [sitting / walking / car — pick whichever suits the energy;
walking suits stories, sitting suits numbers]

## 1 CLAIM
[The single argument, one sentence.]
*Gates: arguable → [who would push back] · sees it → [the object/number/moment they'll picture] ·
one claim → [what you cut, if anything]*

## 2 HOOKS

**Audio hook — pick the one that sounds like you**
1. [~15-20 words. States the claim. A statement, not a question. "You" in it. Aimed at their
   fear / concern / want / need.]
2. [...]
3. [...]

**Visual hook — on screen at frame one, held ~3 seconds**
"[≤8 words]"
*Partners with the audio by: [stating the thesis the story is withholding · compressing the claim
to ≤8 words · naming the situation ("When you…") · repeating the first line verbatim, only if that
line already IS the claim]*
[If they have a real artifact: **Hold up:** the document / receipt / screenshot / chart]

## 3 PILLARS
[Credential, only if they have one — ONE sentence spoken right after your hook and before
Pillar 1, never first: "but first, I'm X, I've done Y."]

**Pillar 1 · [story | number | conversation]** — [outline: one-line cue with any number, name or
phrase they must not forget. | script: the word-for-word block.]

   ↳ **Re-hook:** "[one short line that resets attention and pulls them into Pillar 2]"

**Pillar 2 · [story | number | conversation]** — [...]

   ↳ **Re-hook:** "[one short line that pulls them into Pillar 3]"

**Pillar 3 · [story | number | conversation]** — [...]
[One strong pillar can carry the video — only write 2 or 3 if the notes support them. Cut any
re-hook that has no pillar after it.]

## LAND IT — your last line
"[Option 1 — the exact last line. Write it, memorize it: it's the guardrail that stops you
rambling and your cue to stop talking.]"
"[Option 2 — a different landing: a punchline, a question to the comments, or an open loop (a
tease into the next video).]"
[+ the follow or CTA ask riding AFTER the line, only if they have one — never instead of it]

## DELIVERY NOTES
- [2–4 specific notes: where to pause, where energy spikes, what carries which section]
- Record each pillar 3–5 times; the last take usually sounds the most human.
- Re-record your first line at the end — you'll be warmed up and it shows.
```

**Script mode** keeps the identical headings — the Pillars just contain word-for-word text in
1–2 sentence read-and-repeat chunks (blank line between each, `(pause)` marking a held pause)
instead of one-line cues.

Then offer, in one line: script the other claims found in their notes, a different hook set, or
convert outline ↔ script.

## ANTI-PATTERNS — never produce these

- **Naming a concept instead of pointing at a thing.** The single clearest failure mode.
- **Neutral info-dumps and process walkthroughs** with no judgment welded to the front.
- **Motivational abstraction.** Same creator: 0.2x for motivation, 47–87x for a scene.
- **Inventory lists** ("5 things to look for") — 0.8x. **Judgment lists** ("5 things I notice
  about people that tell me everything") — 47x. Each item must be a verdict, not a fact.
- **Leading with credentials, location, or a greeting.**
- **Opening on a question.**
- **Status broadcasts** — my numbers, my office, my culture. The viewer gets nothing.
- **Reciting parallel scenarios.** Number density without a consequence is a reliable flop.
- **Acted skits and roleplay.** A different format with a different job — if they ask for one,
  say so and build it as a skit, not as a yap.

## EXPECTATION SETTING

If they seem to be judging themselves on one video, tell them the base rate: across 799 videos,
about **1 in 5 beat the creator's own average**, and the *same yap* posted twice a day apart
did 95,628 plays and then 5. Distribution is noisy and not fully in their control. They're building a catalog, not
buying a lottery ticket. Volume is the strategy.
